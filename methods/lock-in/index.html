Lock-in amplifier¶

The classical instrument, implemented offline. For each excited tone the record is multiplied by a cosine and a sine at that frequency, and the products are low-pass filtered. What survives is that tone's slowly varying complex amplitude, and the ratio of the voltage and current amplitudes is \(Z(f,t)\).

It is the most familiar of the four methods to an experimentalist, and — as shown below — it turns out to be the continuous-time generalisation of STFT rather than a separate idea.

The mathematics¶

Write the response near the \(k\)-th tone as a carrier with a slowly varying phasor:

\[ v(t) \;=\; A_v(t)\,\cos\!\big(\omega_k t + \varphi_v(t)\big), \qquad \omega_k = 2\pi f_k \]

Mixing with the in-phase and quadrature references gives

\[ \begin{aligned} v(t)\cos(\omega_k t) &= \tfrac{A_v}{2}\cos\varphi_v \;+\; \tfrac{A_v}{2}\cos(2\omega_k t + \varphi_v) \\ v(t)\sin(\omega_k t) &= -\tfrac{A_v}{2}\sin\varphi_v \;+\; \tfrac{A_v}{2}\sin(2\omega_k t + \varphi_v) \end{aligned} \]

Each product has a baseband term carrying the information and an image at \(2\omega_k\). A low-pass filter \(h\) that rejects the image leaves

\[ X = \tfrac{A_v}{2}\cos\varphi_v, \qquad Y = -\tfrac{A_v}{2}\sin\varphi_v \]

and twice the complex combination is the phasor itself:

\[ V(t) \;=\; 2\big(X - \mathrm{j}Y\big) \;=\; A_v(t)\,e^{\mathrm{j}\varphi_v(t)} \]

The same is done for the current, and

\[ Z(f_k, t) \;=\; \frac{V(t)}{I(t)} \]

The factor of two and the one-sided convention both cancel in that ratio, so they matter only if you want \(V\) and \(I\) to be meaningful on their own.

Choosing the integrator — the whole method¶

The low-pass filter has two jobs. Rejecting the image at \(2f_k\) is easy. The binding requirement is the other one: every other tone of the multisine, after mixing at \(f_k\), lands at the difference frequency \(|f_j - f_k|\), and must be rejected too.

For a multisine whose tones are all harmonics of a base frequency \(f_b\), every such difference is a multiple of \(f_b\). A boxcar average over exactly an integer number of periods of \(f_b\) has spectral nulls at every multiple of \(1/(N_w \Delta t)\) — that is, at every one of those difference frequencies. It rejects them all, exactly.

integrator worst rejection of the other 12 tones
boxcar, 1 base period 307 dB (arithmetic limit)
boxcar, 2 base periods 311 dB
Hann, 2 base periods 116 dB
Hann, 4 base periods 134 dB

(example 08; the boxcar rows are round-off, not a real finite rejection)

tone_rejection includes the tone you demodulated

Each row it returns has one entry per frequency, and the entry for \(f_k\) itself has zero offset and therefore 0 dB. Drop it before taking a worst case, or the answer is always 0 dB.

The window must span whole base periods of \(f_b\), not of \(f_k\)

The nulls sit at multiples of \(1/(N_w\Delta t)\) and only land on the harmonics if \(N_w \Delta t\) is a whole number of base periods. Rounding \(N_w\) to an odd number to centre the kernel on a sample detunes them: on a 20 000-sample period that one extra sample costs about 230 dB of rejection. integrator_length therefore returns the exact length and refuses a non-integer one. The resulting half-sample group delay is common to voltage and current and cancels in their ratio.

A Hann window has its nulls in the same places but a mainlobe twice as wide, so over a single base period the nearest harmonic falls inside the mainlobe and is not rejected at all. integrator_length refuses window='hann' with n_periods=1 for that reason.

The integrator length is also the time resolution: a window of \(m\) base periods cannot follow anything faster than \(m/f_b\) seconds. That is the same trade STFT makes with its window and DMFA with its bandwidth.

Two demodulators¶

Mixing (method='mix')¶

The textbook route described above. Two FFT convolutions per frequency, so the whole record at every sample costs a couple of seconds.

Local least squares (method='ls')¶

Instead of mixing, fit

\[ x(t) \approx a\cos\omega_k\tau + b\sin\omega_k\tau + c + d\,\tau, \qquad \tau = t - t_0 \]

in a sliding window, and take \(a - \mathrm{j}b\) as the phasor. Carrying the offset \(c\) and the local slope \(d\) is the point: the fit tracks drift within the window instead of averaging over it.

That matters more than it sounds. On the transistor system:

demodulator median relative error
mixing, 1-period boxcar 1.16e-03
local least squares, same window 1.05e-04

(valid instants only; example 08)

An order of magnitude, from the same data and the same window, because the within-window averaging bias is what limits the mixing route on a drifting system. The cost is one least-squares solve per reported instant, so it must be evaluated on a decimated grid.

The phasor is referenced to the window centre

local_ls centres \(\tau\) on the window, so the phase it returns is relative to \(t_0\), not to \(t=0\). The reference cancels in \(V/I\) because both are fitted on the same window — which is why lia_impedance_ls is correct — but a caller using local_ls on its own must undo the \(e^{-\mathrm{j}\omega t_0}\) itself.

Calling it¶

from deistools.processing import MultiFrequencyAnalysis

mfa = MultiFrequencyAnalysis(frequencies, voltage, current, sampling_time)

Z, t = mfa.run_lia(n_periods=1, decimate=200)        # mixing
Z, t = mfa.run_lia(n_periods=1, method="ls", stride=20000)   # local LS

mfa.lia_tone_rejection(1)     # dB, one row per demodulated tone
mfa.lia_valid_mask()          # False where the window ran past the record

Or directly:

from deistools.processing.lock_in_amp import integrator_length, lia_eis

samples_window = integrator_length(sampling_time, base_frequency=1/Tp,
                                   n_periods=1, window="boxcar")
Z, t = lia_eis(time, voltage, current, sampling_time, frequencies,
               samples_window, decimate=200)

Edges¶

The output within half an integrator length of either end rests on a window that extends past the data. This implementation reflects the signal at the boundaries, which is exact for an envelope with zero slope there and good otherwise, and valid_mask marks the affected samples so a fit can exclude them.

This is the lock-in's version of DMFA's edge problem, but it is milder and explicitly controllable, because the filtering happens in the time domain where the boundary can be handled directly instead of being wrapped around by a circular DFT.

Worked example¶

examples/08_lock_in_first_run.py runs all of the above on the transistor system: designing the integrator, the rejection table, the estimate, the edges, the agreement with STFT, and the two demodulators side by side.

It is STFT, evaluated everywhere¶

A boxcar lock-in over exactly one base period and a rectangular STFT over the same window are the same estimator. Verified on the transistor record:

max |Z_lia − Z_stft| / |Z_stft|  =  9e-14      (at the STFT window centres)

STFT is that estimator decimated to one point per window; the lock-in reports at every sample. So the lock-in buys no accuracy over STFT at the instants STFT reports — its median error over all instants is higher (1.2e-03 against 7.1e-04) precisely because it also reports between the centres, where the within-window bias is larger.

What it buys is a continuous output, an explicit and adjustable integrator, and — through method='ls' — a way to remove the within-window bias that neither STFT nor plain mixing can.

When to use it¶

  • You want the familiar instrument. This is what a hardware lock-in does, so the parameters mean what an experimentalist expects them to mean.
  • You want a continuous \(Z(f,t)\) rather than one value per window.
  • Your system drifts within a window — use method='ls', which is the only estimator here that removes that bias without assuming a global model the way BLTVA does.
  • You are heading towards online processing. The mixing route is the one that becomes a streaming filter, though a causal integrator would replace the zero-phase one used here.

Limits¶

  • Time resolution is the integrator length, with the same floor as STFT: one base period.
  • No uncertainty is reported, as with STFT and DMFA.
  • Cost grows linearly with the number of tones, since each is demodulated separately. DMFA transforms once for all of them.
