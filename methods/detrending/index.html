Detrending¶

A measured cell drifts. Its open-circuit voltage wanders, its temperature moves, a reference electrode ages — and the resulting slow excursion can be tens of volts peak-to-peak on a signal of one volt. That drift is not what the measurement is for, so the instinct is to filter it out first and estimate afterwards.

The instinct is wrong, and this page is mostly about why. Four strategies are implemented. Each one is useless or harmful with three of the four estimators, and the pattern is not arbitrary: it falls out of one fact about what a filter can do.

The one fact¶

A filter applied by convolution is diagonal in frequency. It multiplies bin \(k\) by \(F(k)\) and does nothing else. So if a trend and the signal both have content at bin \(k\), they are scaled by the same number:

\[ \frac{|T(k)|}{|S(k)|} \quad\text{is unchanged, for every } k \text{ and every diagonal filter} \]

Measured here as bit-identical before and after, for both a boxcar and a Butterworth. A filter cannot separate a trend from the signal where the two overlap, because at a single bin there is nothing to separate them with. It can only reject a disturbance that lives somewhere else in frequency.

Whether a trend overlaps is not a matter of taste. A trend that takes the whole record to unfold has its energy in the lowest few bins — exactly where the slowest excited tones sit. For a typical multisine, the lowest three to five tones are inside the drift's skirt. For those tones, no filter helps.

A projection is not gentler than a filter

It is tempting to think a polynomial fit leaves the excited bins alone, because a polynomial is "slow" and the tones are not. It does not. A degree-\(q\) Legendre polynomial on a finite record is not band-limited: its spectrum reaches every bin, decaying only as a power law. So the projection removes signal at the excited bins, and measurably more than a filter does.

Measured on the stationary cell of example 00, whose impedance does not move, so every change at an excited bin is signal destroyed:

strategy lowest tone top tone
MovingAverage(20001) 5.0e-05 5.0e-05
LowPassRemoval(2, 2) 1.9e-03 2.4e-13
PolynomialFit(1) 6.5e-03 5.3e-05
PolynomialFit(3) 2.1e-02 1.8e-04
PolynomialFit(8) 8.8e-02 5.0e-04
PolynomialFit(12) 1.4e-01 2.7e-04

(example 09)

The damage falls off as \(1/h\) with harmonic number — 6.5e-03, 2.2e-03, 1.3e-03, 7.3e-04 at \(h = 1, 3, 5, 9\), whose product with \(h\) is constant to two figures — which is the spectrum of a ramp. So it is worst at the lowest tones: exactly the ones the detrending was for.

Two things can work:

  • Annihilate the trend in the time domain. \(\Delta^d\) applied to a polynomial of degree \(d-1\) gives exactly zero, pointwise. No multiplication in frequency can do that, and differencing achieves it by not being diagonal — the zero-padded history is what buys the extra power.
  • Model the trend and fit it jointly. Put trend regressors in the design matrix alongside the signal model and let least squares apportion the shared bins. This is what BLTVA's \(N_q\) block does.

Everything below is a consequence.

Which strategy, which estimator¶

strategy BLTVA DMFA / STFT
Differencing the only one that works — though plain \(N_q\) usually beats it destroys the signal (error 1.00)
MovingAverage refused at best 1.7×, and not consistently
LowPassRemoval refused no help on an overlapping trend
PolynomialFit refused — use \(N_q\) the only one that reliably helps

The two columns are exact complements, and one question decides both: can the estimator undo an attenuation?

BLTVA can. Its cost function is weighted by \(W = (FF^H)^{-1}\), so whatever the filter took out is divided back in. That is what lets differencing trade away the entire low-frequency band and still get the answer. DMFA and STFT have no such step, so for them whatever a filter removes is simply gone — and differencing, which removes almost everything near DC, is catastrophic rather than free.

Never pass Differencing to DMFA or STFT

Differencing(1) attenuates the lowest excited bin of a 160 000-sample record to \(|F| = 3\times10^{-4}\). The measured DMFA error at that tone is 1.00 — the tone is gone. In BLTVA the same attenuation costs nothing. This is the most dangerous thing in the module, and the reason frequency_response is a mandatory part of the protocol: it is the only way to see the \(3\times10^{-4}\) coming.

Detrending for BLTVA is not a preprocessing step¶

Removing a trend before a BLTVA fit changes four things at once (2022 paper, eq. 18–27):

  1. every skirt regressor is multiplied by \(F(k)\) — eq. (21)
  2. the cost function is weighted by \(W = (FF^H)^{-1}\) — eq. (26)
  3. the filter's boundary effects add their own regressors — eq. (21)
  4. the transient order \(N_a\) shrinks by the filter order — eq. (22)

So a strategy is not "a function returning a cleaned signal". It is the bundle of those four things, which is what the Detrending protocol encodes. A strategy that cannot report its frequency_response cannot be used, because step 2 would be impossible.

Why the weighting rules out three of the four¶

Step 2 is also what disqualifies them. For a real, zero-phase response \(F/|F| = 1\), so the weighted system is identical to the one built from the raw record — verified to \(5.6\times10^{-17}\) on the skirt regressors. Nothing about the trend was removed. What did change is that the trend block of eq. (58) is gone, replaced by nothing.

Measured on the 2022 paper's own trend, with \(F(k) = 1\) exactly — a filter that does nothing at all, which the protocol happily accepts:

configuration error at the lowest tone
no trend, \(N_q = 5\) 2.2e-04
trend, \(N_q = 5\), no detrending 1.6e-02
trend, \(F(k) = 1\) "detrending" 2.6e+00

A moving average scores well in the same test (1.8e-04), which looks like a refutation until the averaging length is varied:

length error at the lowest tone
101 1.83e-04
401 1.82e-04
1601 1.68e-04

An answer that does not depend on the averaging length is not coming from the averaging. It comes from the \(1/|F(k)|\) weighting applied to the transient block, which eq. (21) does not multiply by \(F\): since \(F \sim k^2\) near DC for any boxcar, the weighting hands the transient polynomial a \(k^{-2}\) envelope, and that reshaped basis happens to absorb the trend skirt. The trend removal is an accident of the reparametrisation — and a Butterworth low-pass, same protocol but a different \(1/|F|\) shape, scores 2.6e-02, worse than no detrending at all.

So BLTVA refuses them, with an error naming the alternative.

The strategies¶

Differencing¶

\[ y_d(n) = \Delta^d \tilde y(n), \qquad \tilde y(n) = 0 \text{ for } n < 0 \]

High-pass, eq. (12b): \(F_d(k) = (1 - z_k^{-1})^d\) with \(z_k = e^{j2\pi k/N}\).

The published strategy, and the only one BLTVA accepts. It suppresses a smooth trend without modelling it, which is the point of the 2022 paper: a trend that no finite polynomial captures cannot be removed parametrically, and trying inflates the variance of the low-frequency estimates.

Two properties make it work where the others fail. It annihilates a degree-\((d-1)\) polynomial identically in the time domain, which no diagonal filter can. And its \(F\) is complex, so \(F/|F|\) is a phase ramp the magnitude weighting does not cancel.

The price is the boundary term — the zero-padded history leaves a polynomial of degree \(d-1\) in \(z_k^{-1}\) (Theorem 2), which becomes the extra regressors of step 3. select_differencing_order picks \(d\) by eq. (11), with a documented caveat: the criterion assumes "trend plus random series", and a strongly low-pass response breaks that, so it can recommend differencing a record with no trend at all. Treat it as a starting point.

What it buys is columns, not accuracy¶

The strategy to beat is no detrending at all, with a generous \(N_q\) — and on accuracy differencing usually loses to it, including on the non-polynomial trend the 2022 paper chose precisely to motivate differencing:

\(N_p\) \(N_q\) cap from eq. (49) best \(N_q\) Differencing(1)
2 17 1.4e-04 (\(N_q=7\)) 8.2e-03 \(N_q\) wins 60×
4 13 2.2e-04 (\(N_q=11\)) 5.5e-04 \(N_q\) wins 2.5×
6 9 1.2e-04 (\(N_q=8\)) 7.1e-02 \(N_q\) wins 588×
8 5 1.9e+00 (\(N_q=4\)) 4.6e-02 differencing wins 42×

(relative error at the lowest excited tone; the paper's trend, 92 V p-p)

The paper's own comparison is the \(N_q = 2\) column of this picture: at that order parametric removal does fail, by 1.35 against differencing's 7.8e-03. Raise \(N_q\) and the conclusion reverses, because a joint fit does not distort and a larger basis simply models the trend better.

What flips the table at \(N_p = 8\) is eq. (49), \(N_q + 3N_p + N_a + 1 < 2P - 1\). A high \(N_p\) — fine time resolution — eats the budget, capping \(N_q\) at 5, and at that order the trend is unmodellable: \(N_q = 4\) gives 1.9e+00, \(N_q = 3\) gives 5.4e+00. Differencing replaces the whole \(N_q\) block with \(d-1\) boundary columns — 15 columns per band instead of 20, for \(N_p=2, N_q=5, N_a=8\) — and those freed columns are the entire point. The \(N_p = 8\) configuration is not otherwise broken: on a trend-free record it reaches 2.0e-04.

So: reach for a generous \(N_q\) first. Use differencing when eq. (49) will not let you afford the trend order you need, which in practice means when you want fine time resolution.

MovingAverage¶

Subtract a centred boxcar average of \(L\) samples. The response is one minus the Dirichlet kernel:

\[ F(k) = 1 - \frac{\sin(\pi k L/N)}{L \sin(\pi k /N)} \]

\(L\) must be odd, so the window has a true centre and \(F\) carries no phase.

A boxcar is a poor filter and this is where it shows. \(F\) has sidelobes, overshoots above 1, and is not monotone — so checking \(|F|\) at a tone is worthless. MovingAverage(20001) on a 160 000-sample record has \(|F| = 1.00005\) at bin 8 and swings from 0.363 to 1.214 over the \(\pm 4\) bins either side, a 3.3× ripple. That band is exactly what DMFA reconstructs the time variation from, so the ripple is an uncorrected envelope distortion. Use band_response and judge by ripple, never by the centre value.

Measured on DMFA at the lowest tone, against a clean baseline of 4.1e-03:

record no filter with it
no trend 4.1e-03 1.9e-02
trended, \(L = 20001\) 8.7e-02 5.0e-02
trended, \(L = 40001\) 8.7e-02 9.7e-02

Those three rows have to be read together. \(L = 20001\) does improve the trended record, by 1.7× — per-bin invariance does not forbid that, because a filter can still reweight which bins dominate a band fit even though it cannot change the ratio within any one of them. But \(L = 40001\) makes it worse, so the direction is not consistent across settings, and the win is paid for by raising the trend-free floor 4.5×. Nothing is gained here that PolynomialFit does not gain better, and more predictably, on the same record.

Present for completeness; LowPassRemoval is strictly the better shape.

LowPassRemoval¶

Subtract the low-pass content, with a Butterworth magnitude:

\[ F(k) = \frac{r^n}{\sqrt{1 + r^{2n}}}, \qquad r = |f_k| / f_c \]

Defined by this magnitude and applied as such, so it is zero-phase and has no startup transient — unlike the same Butterworth run as a time-domain recursion, which would have both and would then need boundary regressors it cannot afford.

The cutoff is in cycles per record, which is the DFT bin index: bin \(k\) is \(k/T\) Hz. That keeps the strategy independent of the sampling rate, and it is the only unit frequency_response(k, N) can resolve. A cutoff of 3 means "treat anything slower than three cycles over the whole record as trend".

Unlike the boxcar it is monotone and never exceeds 1, so a cutoff a few times below the slowest tone gives a genuinely flat band: at cutoff = 2 over \(\pm 4\) bins around bin 8, ripple 1.03× and a DMFA error of 5.8e-03 on a trend-free record against the 4.1e-03 baseline.

It still does not remove a trend that overlaps the tones — 8.7e-02 before, 8.8e-02 after at cutoff = 2, and 1.2e-01 at cutoff = 4, order = 6. What it does well is remove a trend genuinely slower than everything excited, which is the case the cutoff describes. Whether your trend is that case is an empirical question; band_response answers it.

PolynomialFit¶

\[ y_d = \left(I - b (b^Tb)^{-1} b^T\right) y \]

with \(b\) the Legendre basis on the record — the same basis BLTVA uses for its trend block. Legendre rather than monomial because the monomial Vandermonde is hopeless beyond degree ~15.

This is the one strategy with no frequency response at all, and not for want of deriving it. Subtracting a least-squares fit is an orthogonal projection: a rank-\((q+1)\) operator whose frequency-domain kernel is \(\sum_q B_q(k) B_q(k')^*\), dense in \((k,k')\). It mixes bins, so no \(F(k)\) exists, step 2 cannot be carried out, and frequency_response raises.

Being non-diagonal is also exactly what makes it the only strategy that helps DMFA or STFT. On a trended record it takes the lowest tone from 8.7e-02 to 7.9e-03, where both diagonal filters leave it at 8.8e-02 or worse.

The price is paid silently, and it is the thing to understand before using it. Applying the same subtraction to a record with no trend — so that everything removed is signal — gives, at the lowest tone against a clean error of 4.1e-03:

degree DMFA error at the lowest tone
1 4.9e-03
3 7.9e-03
8 4.0e-02
12 6.2e-02

The distortion grows with the degree and there is nothing to correct it with. That is both why the 2022 paper reaches for differencing and why BLTVA fits this basis instead of subtracting it. Keep the degree as low as the trend allows, and read the result as a trade rather than a cleanup.

Inside BLTVA, use Nq instead

The \(N_q\) drift block of eq. (58) is this same Legendre basis, fitted jointly with the impedance rather than subtracted first. The trend is then separated from the signal using the whole band of data, and the distortion above does not arise at all. PolynomialFit exists for the estimators that have no such block, and for measuring what its absence costs.

Worked example¶

examples/09_detrending.py runs all four strategies on one drifting record and plots what the user usually wants to see first: the original and detrended signals, and the effect on the DFT spectrum. It also reproduces the three tables on this page — the trend-to-signal ratio per bin, the damage to the excited bins, and the DMFA error before and after — and shows BLTVA accepting exactly one of the four.

The record there has 8 periods rather than the 20 used elsewhere, which puts the lowest tone at bin 8 instead of bin 20 and so much closer to the drift's skirt. That is the situation detrending exists for, and it is where the numbers on this page come from.

Checking a strategy¶

check_response verifies step 1 of the protocol — that apply is what frequency_response claims:

\[ \mathrm{DFT}(\texttt{apply}(y)) = F(k)\,\mathrm{DFT}(y) + \big(\text{something in the span of } \texttt{extra\_regressors}\big) \]

It returns the part of the residual the boundary regressors cannot absorb. All four implemented strategies return \(\sim 10^{-15}\):

strategy residual
Differencing(1) 2.9e-15
Differencing(4) 9.6e-16
MovingAverage(401) 6.8e-13
LowPassRemoval(3, 2) 4.2e-16

Anything larger means the strategy is reporting a response that does not describe what it did, and an estimator built on it is biased with no warning. A constant column joins the projection, because the estimator has one: eq. (21) omits the \(m=0\) boundary term on the grounds that the transient polynomial already spans a constant.

This is the test that decides which category a new strategy falls into, and it is worth running on anything added later.

In practice¶

  • A polynomial trend, with BLTVA. Use Nq. Nothing else, no detrending.
  • A trend no polynomial captures, with BLTVA. Still try \(N_q\) first, as generously as eq. (49) allows — it beat Differencing(1) by 60× on the 2022 paper's own trend. Reach for Differencing(d) when \(N_p\) has eaten the budget and the \(N_q\) cap is below what the trend needs, with \(d\) from select_differencing_order as a starting point, checked against the spectrum. See what it buys.
  • Any trend, with DMFA or STFT. PolynomialFit at the lowest degree that works, accepting the distortion in the table above — or, for the tones the drift actually overlaps, use BLTVA instead. The DMFA page has the measurements and the joint-solve alternative.
  • To study what naive detrending costs, apply the strategy yourself and call bltva with detrend=None. That is the honest way to reproduce what a user who filters first would get, and it needs no escape hatch.
