DMFA implementation notes¶

Verification status

The definition and the filter have now been checked line by line against Battistel et al. (2016), Koster et al. (2017) and Battistel and La Mantia (2019), and both match — see below. What is still open is an end-to-end comparison against an independent implementation on the same data.

One finding from that check is a caveat the papers do not state: the mirroring they recommend for non-periodic records must not be applied to a random-phase multisine. See below.

Which definition is implemented¶

Three definitions appear in this literature, and they are not equivalent.

Battistel 2016, eq. (11) writes the dynamic impedance as the stationary ratio times a correction,

\[ Z_k(t) = \frac{V_k}{I_k}\left\{1 - \sum_{l=1}^{bw} \left[\frac{I_{k,l}}{I_k}e^{\,2\pi \mathrm{j} l \Delta f t} + \frac{I_{k,l}}{I_k}e^{-2\pi \mathrm{j} l \Delta f t}\right]\right\} \]

This is a first-order expansion, valid only while the skirt is small against its carrier. The odd look of it — a series that has to be truncated, with the truncation doubling as the bandwidth — is real, and it is why the later papers replaced it.

Koster 2017, eq. (9) is the clean statement: the ratio of two filtered inverse transforms, with no expansion and no smallness assumption. This is what DEIStools implements, and it is the current definition.

Battistel 2019, eq. (4) goes further and gives the physical definition via a Volterra series, against which both heuristics can be judged. It is a reference point rather than an algorithm; the paper finds DMFA within about 1% of it at the lowest frequency and below 0.1% at the highest.

So: the calculation that looked odd was the 2016 one, and it is not the one in this package.

The filter matches the published one up to a constant¶

create_fermi_dirac_fun evaluates

\[ \tilde g = \frac{\cosh n}{\cosh n + \cosh (n x)}, \qquad x = \frac{f - f_c}{bw} \]

while Battistel 2019 eq. (12) and Chukwu 2022 eq. (3) specify the product of two Fermi-Dirac functions, which simplifies exactly to

\[ \tilde g_{\text{paper}} = \frac{\cosh n + 1}{\cosh n + \cosh (n x)} \]

Checked numerically: the two agree in shape to 1e-14, and their ratio is the frequency-independent constant

\[ \frac{\tilde g_{\text{DEIStools}}}{\tilde g_{\text{paper}}} = \frac{\cosh n}{\cosh n + 1} = 0.999329524658 \quad (n = 8) \]

Since DMFA divides voltage by current through the same filter, the constant cancels exactly and the impedance is unaffected. The only consequence is that the DEIStools filter has gain 0.99933 at the centre rather than 1, which matters if you use the filter for anything that is not a ratio.

Both conventions put \(\tilde g = 1/2\) at \(x = \pm 1\), so bw means the same thing in both: the half-amplitude half-width.

The one-sided extraction is correct¶

The papers stress that \(\tilde g\) is a quadrature filter, defined only for positive frequencies, because that is what makes the inverse transform the analytic signal — the complex envelope — rather than a real band-limited signal.

extract_impedance takes a block of bins around \(+f_k\) only and never the mirror at \(-f_k\), so the output is inherently one-sided. That is correct and deliberate. The resulting factor of \(1/2\) (a real cosine carries half its energy on each side) cancels in the ratio, as does the Npts_elab scaling.

Block extraction is a controlled approximation¶

Here DEIStools departs from the papers' procedure, for cost. The papers filter the whole spectrum and inverse-transform at full length; extract_impedance takes only Npts_elab bins around each tone and inverse-transforms those. That is equivalent to filtering the full spectrum with a filter forced to zero outside the block, so the two agree only to the extent that the filter has already decayed at the block edge.

Measured on the transistor record at bw = 0.5 f_min, n = 2:

block width filter value at the edge difference from full-spectrum filtering (median / worst)
4 bandwidths 1.2e-01 1.1e-03 / 2.3e-02
8 bandwidths 2.5e-03 1.5e-05 / 2.6e-03
16 bandwidths 8.5e-07 4.5e-09 / 9.2e-07

The error tracks the edge value almost exactly, as it should.

Use at least 16 bandwidths

The examples size the block at 8 bandwidths, which with a soft filter (n = 2) leaves a worst-case 2.6e-03 — the same order as the method's own error. Sixteen costs nothing but arithmetic and puts the truncation at 1e-09. A sharper filter needs a narrower block; the rule is to extend the block until the filter has fallen below about 1e-06.

Mirroring must not be used with a random-phase multisine¶

Battistel 2019 §3.1 recommends mirroring a non-periodic record about its last point, doubling its length, to suppress the Gibbs skirt. Applied to a random-phase multisine it is destructive, and badly so.

Mirroring reflects the record in time. For a tone \(\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\) occupying a whole number of periods, the reflection is \(\cos(\omega t - \varphi)\): the cosine part is even and survives, but the sine part is folded into \(\sin(\omega|t|)\), which has a derivative kink at the seam and therefore a broadband spectrum. The scattered energy lands in every extraction band.

Measured on the standard 13-tone grid:

record off-bin / on-bin energy
original 1.1e-24
mirrored, phases all zero 6.2e-04
mirrored, random phases 1.5e+00

With random phases the multisine scatters more energy off its bins than it leaves on them. The end-to-end effect on the impedance is what you would expect:

worst error, edges included
plain 4.1e-01
mirrored 7.5e+00

The paper's own case is different: their multisine was periodic and it was the cyclic voltammetry that was not, so mirroring fixed the dc skirt without touching the tones. The technique is sound there and wrong here.

Conclusion: discard the edges. It is the robust answer, it costs a fixed fraction of the record, and it is what the examples do. If you need the edges, the lock-in filters in the time domain, where the boundary can be reflected on the smooth baseband envelope instead of being wrapped around by a circular DFT.

Is the Fermi-Dirac filter the right choice? No.¶

The Fermi-Dirac distribution comes from statistical physics, not from signal processing, and the natural question is whether a standard DSP window does better. It does. Scored against known ground truth, the published filter comes last of seven, and the best standard window is about twice as good on the worst case.

This corrects an earlier conclusion on this page

A first comparison here tested only the raised-cosine (Tukey) family and reported that the Fermi-Dirac won. That comparison was incomplete: the cosine-sum windows — Blackman, Blackman-Harris, Nuttall — and Kaiser were not in it, and they are the ones that beat it. The table below supersedes it.

What the filter actually does to the result¶

In a galvanostatic measurement the current is the excitation: exactly periodic, so its envelope is constant and the filter merely scales it. All the time variation sits in the voltage. The consequence is that

\[ Z_{\text{measured}}(f_k, t) \;\approx\; \frac{\big(\tilde g * Z\big)(f_k, t)}{\tilde g(0)} \]

— the reported impedance is the true \(Z(f_k,t)\) convolved with the filter's time-domain kernel. So the filter's frequency response is, directly, the transfer function applied to the time variation, and its time-domain sidelobes are the ringing: at the record edges, and wherever the variation turns.

That is precisely the quantity the classical window designs optimise, which is why they win.

The measurement¶

Every candidate scaled to 1/2 at \(|f - f_c| = bw\) so that the bandwidth means the same thing in each, every bandwidth swept, the band extracted 24 bandwidths wide and the output decimated to the independent spacing. Scored by worst-case relative error over the usable part of the record:

filter transistor legendre block-oriented mean rank
blackman 2.40e-04 5.21e-04 1.18e-01 2.7
kaiser, β = 8 2.92e-04 5.94e-04 1.18e-01 3.0
blackman-harris 2.96e-04 6.94e-04 1.18e-01 3.7
DPSS, NW = 4 2.97e-04 7.09e-04 1.18e-01 4.0
hann (= raised cosine α=1) 4.71e-04 9.67e-04 1.17e-01 4.3
gaussian 3.05e-04 9.77e-04 1.18e-01 4.7
Fermi-Dirac, n = 2 4.98e-04 6.47e-04 1.27e-01 5.7

Three systems, and the ordering is stable on the two that discriminate. The block-oriented column is effectively a tie because its error there is dominated by the branch filters' roll-off rather than by the extraction — see example 23.

Two details worth noting:

  • DPSS lands on top of Kaiser, within 1%, which is the expected result: Kaiser is a closed-form approximation to the first Slepian sequence. Since time concentration is what decides this problem, the provably optimal concentration was the obvious thing to try, and it confirms that Kaiser is already at that limit. It is not offered in the package because it needs an eigensolve for no measurable gain.
  • A rectangular filter — eq. (13) of the paper — is catastrophic, at 3.8e-02. The smooth filter of eq. (12) was a large improvement over it; it simply was not taken as far as the window literature already had.

What n actually controls¶

A second correction. At n = 8 the Fermi-Dirac is a flat top with a steep roll-off. At n = 2 — the value the bandwidth/sharpness sweep selects — it is not flat at all, but a smooth bell. Calling n a sharpness parameter is misleading: it trades passband flatness against time concentration, and on this problem concentration is what pays. That is also why the two least flat-topped candidates came out ahead of the flatter ones.

A metric that did not survive scrutiny

An earlier version of this page quoted a "time concentration" figure — the fraction of kernel energy beyond \(5/bw\) — and drew conclusions from it. That number turned out to depend on the frequency grid it was evaluated on, moving by orders of magnitude between two reasonable choices, and the ranking it implied did not match the end-to-end result. It has been removed. The error against ground truth is the measure that decides.

What this package does about it¶

The Fermi-Dirac remains the default, because it is what the DMFA papers specify and this package's job is to implement the published methods faithfully. But create_window_filter makes every alternative a one-line change:

from deistools.processing.passband_filters import create_window_filter

band_filter = create_window_filter(band_axis, 0, bandwidth, "blackman")

and create_raised_cosine_fun covers the Tukey family. Example 12 runs the comparison, so the ranking can be re-checked on your own system rather than taken on trust — which is the point, since the block-oriented column already shows a case where the filter choice does not matter at all.

DMFA as linear algebra: it is BLTVA in a different basis¶

DMFA looks like a filtering operation and BLTVA looks like a least-squares fit, which makes them seem like different kinds of thing. They are not. DMFA is already a linear-algebra estimator; it just happens to use a basis in which the solve is trivial.

The implicit model¶

Filtering a band of width \(bw\) around a tone and transforming back assumes the envelope is band-limited to \(|f| < bw\). That is a model, and on a record of length \(T\) it is this one:

\[ V_k(t) \;=\; \sum_{m=-M}^{M} c_m \, e^{\,\mathrm{j}2\pi m t / T}, \qquad M = bw\,T \]

— the envelope is a trigonometric polynomial of degree \(bw\,T\), with \(2M+1\) complex coefficients. Compare BLTVA, whose model is

\[ Z(f,t) \;=\; \sum_{p=0}^{N_p} H_p(f)\, b_p(t) \]

with \(b_p\) the Legendre polynomials and \(N_p+1\) coefficients. Same structure, different basis.

Fitting it gives exactly DMFA¶

Build the design matrix in the time domain — one column pair per mode, \(\cos\) and \(-\sin\) of \((\omega_k + 2\pi m/T)\,t\) — and solve the ordinary least-squares problem. The result is the band extraction, to machine precision:

max |DMFA − least squares| / max   =  1.3e-15
design-matrix condition number     =  1.000000

That condition number is the whole explanation. The columns are distinct Fourier harmonics, so they are orthogonal on the record; the normal equations \(K^{\mathrm{H}}K\,\hat\theta = K^{\mathrm{H}}y\) have a diagonal matrix on the left, and the solve degenerates into reading off the DFT bins. There is nothing to invert. BLTVA's Legendre columns are not orthogonal in the band, which is why it has a solve, a condition number worth reporting, and a pruning step.

A smooth filter is ridge regularisation¶

Multiplying coefficient \(m\) by the filter gain \(g_m\) is exactly diagonal Tikhonov shrinkage. Minimising \(\|y - Kc\|^2 + \sum_m \lambda_m |c_m|^2\) gives \(\hat c_m = y_m/(1+\lambda_m)\), so

\[ g_m \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\lambda_m} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \lambda_m \;=\; \frac{1}{g_m} - 1 \]

Verified to 1.2e-15. So the choice between a rectangular and a Fermi-Dirac filter is not a choice between "no filter" and "a filter" — it is a choice between hard model-order truncation and a ridge prior, with the penalty rising smoothly towards the band edge. The filter is the regulariser.

Three things this explains¶

It gives DMFA an uncertainty. A linear estimator \(\hat c = Ay\) has covariance \(A\Sigma A^{\mathrm{H}}\), which is the route BLTVA already takes. Working it through reproduces Chukwu eq. (19)–(20) exactly — their error propagation is this covariance — and extract_impedance_with_error now computes it, agreeing with the scatter over 200 noise realisations to better than 1%.

It explains the independent spacing. The model has \(2\,bw\,T\) free coefficients over a record of length \(T\), so there is one degree of freedom per \(T/(2\,bw\,T) = 1/(2\,bw)\) seconds. That is precisely the \(0.5/bw\) at which neighbouring errors stop being correlated — the rule is just "do not report more points than the model has parameters".

It explains the edges, and points at a better basis. The Fourier basis is periodic by construction. Band-limitedness does not require periodicity; the basis imposes it, and a non-periodic envelope then produces Gibbs oscillation. BLTVA's polynomial basis carries no such constraint, which is why it has no edge problem at all.

The natural basis for band-limited on a finite interval is the Slepian (DPSS) basis, not the Fourier one. Fitting the same band with the same number of coefficients:

basis at \(t=0\) interior at \(t=T\)
Fourier (periodic) 26.8% 1.5% 3.2%
Slepian (not periodic) 5.0% 1.6% 2.4%

Five times better at the leading edge, for nothing — same bandwidth, same degrees of freedom, same data. The interior is unchanged, as it must be.

Not implemented

A Slepian-basis DMFA is a genuine improvement over zero-padding rather than an alternative to it: padding removes the wrap-around, a non-periodic basis removes the reason the wrap hurt. It needs an eigensolve per bandwidth and a different code path from extract_impedance, so it is on the roadmap rather than in the package. The same DPSS appeared in the filter comparison above, for the same underlying reason.

Variance: propagation, or measured in-band?¶

Chukwu et al. (2022) derive the expected variance of the error on the admittance by propagating the measurement noise through the DMFA chain:

\[ \sigma^2_{Y_{l,k_0}} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_i^2 + \big|Y_{l,k_0}\big|^2\,\sigma_v^2} {2\,N'\,\big|\tilde v_{l,k_0}\big|^2}, \qquad N' = \frac{N}{\sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2}\big|\tilde g(k-k_0)\big|^2} \tag{19, 20} \]

The derivation is sound — \(N'\) is the usual equivalent-noise-bandwidth factor, and the assumptions (noise of constant variance, uncorrelated between samples, Parseval for the filter norm) are stated rather than hidden.

The weakness is not the algebra; it is where \(\sigma_i\) and \(\sigma_v\) come from. The paper obtains them from a separate calibration: zero volts across resistors, histogrammed. That assumes the noise is white and that it has not changed between the calibration and the measurement.

The BLTVA route is available and is better¶

BLTVA already estimates the noise variance from the measurement itself, using bins the excitation never reaches (Algorithm 1 of Hallemans TIM 2021, exposed here as estimate_noise_variance). On an odd-harmonic grid with gaps those bins are plentiful, and the estimate:

  • needs no separate instrument calibration;
  • captures the noise colour, not just its level;
  • reflects the noise as it was during that measurement;
  • can be separated from nonlinear distortion by the parity of the bins.

The two are complementary rather than rival. Keep eq. (19) — it is the correct propagation through the filter — and feed it a \(\sigma^2(f)\) measured from the unexcited bins instead of from a resistor. That removes the assumption the formula rests on while keeping its structure. The section above shows that eq. (19) is nothing more than the covariance of a linear estimator, which is also why BLTVA can report one.

extract_impedance_with_error now computes it correctly. Three bugs had survived in that function, unnoticed because it had no test coverage: it returned the reciprocal of the variance (a half-finished edit was still commented out on its last line), it squared arguments already named variance_*, and its N' used the width of the extracted band where eq. (20) calls for the length of the record. Corrected, it agrees with the scatter over 200 noise realisations to 0.3%. Passing it a data-driven noise variance from the unexcited bins, rather than a resistor calibration, is left to the caller and is the remaining improvement.

A testable hypothesis about the paper's own residual

Chukwu et al. report that below 100 Hz their measured standard deviations run up to four times the predicted value, and state the reason could not be ascertained. A noise floor rising towards low frequency — \(1/f\) noise, which is ubiquitous in electrochemical cells — would produce exactly that signature, and a white-noise calibration cannot see it. A data-driven estimate from the unexcited bins would. This has not been tested here and is offered as a hypothesis, not a result.

Do not treat successive instants as independent¶

Neighbouring estimates rest on overlapping filter kernels, so their errors are correlated. Chukwu et al. put the correlation below 20% once successive instants are more than \(0.5/bw\) apart. Measured here on a stationary cell — where the impedance never moves, so the whole spread of the estimate is noise — over 150 noise realisations:

output spacing instants over the record corr(\(e_j\), \(e_{j+1}\)) corr(\(e_j\), \(e_{j+2}\))
0.125/bw 80 84% 50%
0.25/bw 40 49% 4%
0.5/bw 20 3% 0%
1/bw 10 −3% −3%
2/bw 4 −9% −1%

The rule is confirmed, and the earlier examples sat at 0.125/bw — four times too dense, with 84% correlated neighbours. Reporting densely is fine for looking at a result; using every point as an independent observation in a least-squares fit is not, and will make the parameter uncertainties too small.

independent_time_step(bandwidth) returns \(0.5/bw\). Note that Npts_elab sets the band width and the reporting rate together, and they are different concerns — the band must be wide enough for the filter to decay, which is finer than this spacing. Extract wide, then decimate, which is what the examples now do.

The time resolution is not free after all

At the bandwidth that measures best here, \(bw = 0.5 f_{\min}\), the independent spacing works out to \(0.5/bw\) = one multisine period — exactly STFT's window. DMFA's resolution is adjustable where STFT's is fixed, which is a real advantage, but the claim that it is simply finer was an artefact of reporting more densely than the data supports. Buying genuinely finer resolution means widening the band, and example 12 shows what that costs.

The record edges are a property of the method¶

The band-limited inverse transform rings at the record boundaries. Measured on the four simulated systems:

system worst, edges removed worst, edges kept
stationary 1.35e-05 1.35e-05
transistor 1.39e-03 4.13e-01
legendre 1.33e-03 8.85e-01
block-oriented 1.71e-01 4.26e-01

The stationary row is the diagnostic one: identical with and without the edges. A constant impedance occupies a single bin, so truncating the band removes nothing and there is nothing to ring.

That establishes the cause. The artefact is not "the record ended" — it is the cost of representing a changing quantity with a finite band, under a DFT that treats the record as periodic when the envelope is not. No parameter setting removes it. This package discards the first and last tenth throughout.

The filter belongs on the band's local axis¶

elaboration_frequency_axis(n_points, T) returns the frequency axis of an extracted band: n_points bins of width \(1/T\), centred on zero — about 0.2 Hz in the worked example. The full spectrum axis spans \(f_s\), 1000 Hz there.

Build the passband on the wrong axis and its bandwidth is scaled wrongly by \(f_s T / n_{\text{points}}\), a factor of 2500. The result is not an error, just a filter that does something entirely different from what was asked. This is the easiest mistake to make with the method and the reason elaboration_frequency_axis exists as a named function rather than being inlined.

Display fixes in visualize_dmfa_filtering¶

Three defects were corrected in the slider view. They were display-only — no estimation path was affected — but they had survived because the function had no test coverage:

  • the voltage panel drew its "Original data" trace with the filter applied, making it identical to the filtered trace until the slider was first moved;
  • the initial title hard-coded the first frequency rather than the selected one;
  • a stray parenthesis in the updated title (Fouri(er).

The parameter name frequenies is still misspelled in the public signature, so pass that argument positionally.
